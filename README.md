# Code associated with "Double-ITCZ Bias Reduces Southern Hemisphere Influence on Tropics via Oceanic Pathways"
Cassia Cai, Matthew T. Luongo, Anna-Lena Deppenmeier, LuAnne Thompson, William S. Kessler

## Abstract
Many coupled climate models exhibit an Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) south of the Equator in the tropical Pacific that is more pronounced than observed. This bias impacts winds and wind-driven ocean circulation, including the meridional subtropical cells. We compare particle pathways from a double-ITCZ-biased coupled climate simulation with those from an ocean simulation forced by atmospheric reanalysis. This bias influences the zonal distribution of waters supplying the equatorial thermocline. In the forced ocean simulation, Southern Hemisphere (SH) subducted waters travel directly to the equator in the Central and Eastern Pacific. In the coupled simulation, surface winds associated with the South Pacific Convergence Zone intensify the SH subsurface meridional potential vorticity gradient, blocking interior flow and diverting it to western boundary currents. These circulation changes are accompanied by modifications in potential density, altering equatorial water mass properties and potentially the simulation of decadal climate variability.

## Plain Language Summary
Much of predictability of the climate system is linked to basin-scale variability in the tropical Pacific Ocean. Climate models often simulate erroneous wind patterns in the tropical South Pacific that affect ocean circulation features such as the subtropical cells (STCs) that connect the subtropics and the tropics. We compare the STC pathways of ocean water in two climate model simulations: a fully coupled (ocean-atmosphere) model that suffers from too much wind convergence in the southern tropics and an ocean-only model that is forced by realistic winds. Comparisons of tracked water parcels in the two model representations show that these wind biases result in a subsurface barrier in the Southeast Pacific that requires water parcels to travel to the western boundary before they enter the equatorial thermocline. In the Southern Hemisphere, the effect of this barrier is strong and diverts flow to the fast, narrow western boundary currents at the Pacific Ocean basin’s western edge where water properties can be modified before entering the equatorial thermocline. These changes affect the reliability of interannual-to-decadal climate predictions and long-term projections of climate variability.
